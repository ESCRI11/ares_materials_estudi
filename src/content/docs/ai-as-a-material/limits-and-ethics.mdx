This is not the disclaimer at the end of the section. It is the part that changes what you
build, because every limitation below has an interface consequence — something you either
designed for or did not.

## Hallucination is a different problem inside a product

In a chat window, you are the safety mechanism. You read the answer, something looks off, you
check it. That works because you are an expert on your own question and you were already
suspicious.

Put the same model inside something you designed and all three of those disappear:

- **The user did not ask the question**, so they have no expectation to check anything.
- **They cannot see the prompt**, the sources, or that a model was involved at all.
- **Your interface's confidence is your claim**, not the model's. Clean type in a well-designed
  card reads as a fact your product is asserting.

And the arithmetic changes. A wrong answer once a week in your own chat is an annoyance. The
same rate across a thousand users is a wrong answer every few minutes, delivered to people with
no way to know.

The rule this produces is short: **never present a guess as a fact**. Show where it came from,
mark what was generated, and make the answer's status visible without a footnote nobody reads.

## Bias comes with the training data

A model learned from text people wrote, so it carries the shape of who wrote things down, in
which languages, about whom, and which of it was digitised. It reproduces the defaults of that
material: which names sound professional, which occupations get which pronouns, whose English
is "correct", which country is assumed when you say "here".

The second layer is the tuning stage — the human ratings that turn a text-continuer into an
assistant. Those ratings encode the values and guidelines of the people who wrote them, which
is a specific set of people, not a neutral one.

Where this reaches your work is subtler than the obvious cases. Generated personas that all
turn out the same age and class. Research summaries where the unusual participant gets smoothed
into the majority, because averaging is exactly what the mechanism does. Copy written for a
default reader who resembles the training data more than your audience.

You can test for it in an afternoon: run the same prompt with names, cities and contexts from
different places, and read the outputs next to each other. Differences that you did not put in
the prompt came from somewhere.

## Where your users' words go

Anything typed into a hosted model leaves the machine, crosses the network, and is processed on
a company's servers. What happens next depends on the provider and the specific product — and
the terms for a consumer chat app and the same company's API are often not the same document.
Depending on which you use, your text may be retained for a period, reviewed by humans for
abuse, or used to improve the model.

This matters more for you than for most people, because of what design research consists of.

A participant who consented to you recording an interview consented to *you*. Pasting that
transcript into a hosted model is a disclosure to a third party that they were not asked about,
and in Switzerland and the EU that is a data-protection question and not only an etiquette one.
The same applies to a client's unreleased work under NDA, to anything with a face or a real
name in it, and to the messages your users type into a product you shipped.

Three defensible positions, in the order you should consider them:

1. **Do not send it.** Some material has no business leaving your laptop.
2. **Send it anonymised.** Strip names, employers, addresses and anything identifying before
   the model sees it. Do the stripping yourself; do not ask the model to do it.
3. **Run it locally**, which the
   [previous lesson](/ares_materials_estudi/ai-as-a-material/running-a-model-locally/) covers.
   This is the only option where "where did the data go" has a clean answer.

If you are gathering data that will meet a model at some point, say so on the consent form
while you are writing it. It is one sentence then, and an unfixable problem afterwards.

## The costs that are not on the invoice

Two facts, stated once, without a sermon.

**Energy and water.** Training a large model consumes a great deal of electricity, and serving
it consumes more, continuously, in data centres that need cooling. One query is a small amount.
A product that fires off a request on every keystroke, multiplied by its users, is not. Whether
a feature deserves a model call is a design question with an environmental component, and it is
usually answered by noticing that a lookup or a rule would have done.

**Labour.** The tuning and safety layers rest on human work — people rating outputs, labelling
data, and reviewing the worst material the internet produces so that the model refuses to
repeat it. That work is often outsourced, often low-paid, and sometimes genuinely harmful to do.
The polish you experience as effortless was not.

Neither fact means do not use these tools. Both mean use them deliberately, which is the whole
argument of the course title: a material has costs, and a designer who does not know a
material's costs is not designing with it.

## Whose work is in there

Models were trained on enormous quantities of text, images and code whose authors were not
asked and are not credited. Whether that is permitted is being argued in courts in several
countries right now and the answer is not settled — a live legal question rather than a
philosophical one.

Practically, for you:

- **Your work is probably in there**, and so is every designer's you admire.
- **An output can closely resemble a specific source** without any indication that it did.
  Prompting for "in the style of" a living artist is the case where this stops being abstract.
- **Say when a model made something.** In coursework, in a portfolio, to a client. Undisclosed
  generation is the thing that damages trust, more than the generation itself.
- **Check what your client or your school requires**, because policies differ and both exist.

## Designing for the failure case

Here is the part that is actually your job. Below is one card, holding three different things
that came back from a model. Press each button.

Look at the first two. The interface cannot tell them apart, because nothing in the wrong one
looks wrong — it is the true sentence with an invented benchmark welded on. No amount of
styling detects that. What the design can do is refuse to make a claim the model cannot support:
say where it came from, keep the source one click away, and never let the summary replace the
transcripts.

The third case is the one prototypes skip. The model will return nothing, return something
unusable, refuse, or take thirty seconds — in production, on the day of the assessment, never
during your demo. An empty state that explains itself is part of the feature, not a polish task.

Five decisions worth making before you write the prompt:

1. **Suggestion, not action.** The model proposes; the person disposes. Editable, dismissable,
   reversible.
2. **Nothing irreversible unattended.** Sending, publishing, deleting, paying, contacting a real
   person — a human sees it first. This is also the defence against
   [prompt injection](/ares_materials_estudi/ai-as-a-material/calling-a-model/).
3. **Show the provenance.** What was generated, from what, and how to get to the source.
4. **Design the wait, the empty and the refusal**, with the same care as the happy path.
5. **Test the bad path on purpose.** Feed it nonsense, feed it another language, unplug the
   network, force a failure. What your interface looks like when the model is wrong *is* the
   design.

:::tip[One question to hold onto]
If the model is confidently wrong here, what happens to the person in front of it? If the
answer is "they notice and fix it in two seconds", spend your effort elsewhere. If the answer
is "they never find out", that is where the design work is.
:::
