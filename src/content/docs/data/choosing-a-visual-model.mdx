This is the lesson where your training is worth more than a statistician's. Everything here
is about how a mark on a surface is perceived, and you have spent years on that. What you
are adding is a constraint: the shape has to be *true*, and true has a specific meaning when
a length stands for a number.

Two failures are possible, and only one of them looks like a failure. A chart can be ugly,
which everyone notices. A chart can be beautiful and misleading, which nobody notices, and
that is the one this lesson is about.

## Start from the question, not the chart

The syllabus phrase is *identify appropriate visual models*, and the way you identify one is
by writing the question as a sentence first. Nearly every question in a design project is
one of five kinds.

- **Comparison** — "which of these is biggest?" One value per category. A **bar chart**,
  sorted by value rather than alphabetically unless the categories have their own order.
- **Composition** — "what is this made of?" Parts of one whole. A stacked bar, or a set of
  bars showing the parts side by side. Percentages need to add up to a hundred, and if they
  do not, say why.
- **Distribution** — "how are these spread out?" Many measurements of one thing: every wait
  time, every temperature reading. A **histogram** — the values grouped into ranges, one bar
  per range — which shows you the shape, the clumps and the outliers that a single average
  hides completely.
- **Relationship** — "does this move with that?" Two measurements of the same records. A
  **scatter plot**: one dot per record, one variable on each axis.
- **Change over time** — "what happened?" A **line chart**, time on the horizontal axis
  running left to right, because that is the direction everyone already reads it in.

Pick the family from the question and you are most of the way there. The remaining choice —
which of two or three forms in that family — is a design decision, and yours.

## How accurately people read a mark

There is a ranking, established by experiments in the 1980s by Cleveland and McGill and
confirmed many times since. People compare quantities most accurately when they are encoded
as:

1. **Position** along a common scale — dots on the same axis, bar tops on the same baseline
2. **Length** — bars, if they start from the same place
3. **Angle and slope** — the direction of a line, a slice of a pie
4. **Area** — circles, squares, bubbles
5. **Colour intensity** — light to dark
6. **Hue** — red versus blue, which is not read as a quantity at all

The list is not about beauty. It is about error: the further down you go, the further people's
estimates land from the real numbers. You can see the gap between rank 2 and rank 4 directly.

    ctx.fillRect(20 + i * 60, 110 - height, 30, height);
    ctx.fillText(values[i], 20 + i * 60, 126);
  }

  // Area: the radius is proportional to the value. This is the mistake.
  for (let i = 0; i < values.length; i++) {
    ctx.beginPath();

    ctx.fill();
  }
</script>`}

The third value is four times the first. The third bar is four times as long, which is what
you see. The third circle has four times the radius and therefore **sixteen times the area**,
which is what you feel — and area is what the eye reads a circle by, not radius.

If you must size circles by a value, the radius has to be proportional to the square root of
it: `Math.sqrt(value)`. Change `values[i]` to `Math.sqrt(values[i]) * 6` in the second loop
and press Run. The circles now grow honestly, and they are still harder to compare than the
bars, which is the whole argument.

## Why the pie chart is usually wrong

A pie chart encodes with angle and area — ranks 3 and 4 — and asks people to compare slices
that do not share a baseline and are rotated differently from each other. Two slices of 23%
and 26% are indistinguishable in a pie and unmistakable as bars.

It is not forbidden. A pie is readable when there are two or three slices, when they add up
to a meaningful whole, and when "roughly half" is the entire message. Beyond that, the same
data as a sorted bar chart is easier to read, easier to label and takes less space.

The bar chart is dull the way a grid is dull. It is the default because it uses the two most
accurate encodings — position and length — and because everyone can read one without being
taught.

## Honest axes

**A bar chart must start at zero.** The length of the bar *is* the quantity, so cutting the
bottom off multiplies differences: two bars for 98 and 100 on an axis starting at 96 look
like a doubling. This is the most common misleading chart in the world and it is usually an
accident.

**A line chart may start elsewhere**, because it encodes position and slope rather than
length. A body temperature chart starting at zero would be a flat line telling you nothing.
Label the axis clearly so the reader can see where it starts.

Three more that cost nothing:

- **Label both axes, with units.** `duration (s)` rather than `duration`.
- **Do not put two different scales on one chart** with an axis on each side. Any
  relationship you see between the two lines is a consequence of how you scaled them.
- **Keep the spacing even.** Uneven gaps on a time axis — three months, then a year, then a
  week, evenly spaced — distort every slope in the picture.

## Colour, which is two different tools

**Colour for categories.** Different hues for different things: cities, materials, materials
in a taxonomy. Hue carries no order and no magnitude, which is exactly right for categories
and exactly wrong for quantities. Keep it to about seven; beyond that nobody can hold the
legend in their head, and grouping the tail into "other" tells more truth than twelve colours.

**Colour for quantities.** One hue, running light to dark — a **sequential** scale. Light
means less, dark means more, and that reading is close to automatic. When there is a
meaningful midpoint — zero, the average, before and after — use a **diverging** scale: two
hues meeting at a neutral middle.

**Colour-blindness is not an edge case.** Around one in twelve men and one in two hundred
women cannot distinguish some pairs of colours, red and green most often. The rule that
covers it: **never let hue be the only difference**. Vary lightness as well as hue, label
the thing directly instead of sending the reader to a legend, or add a second cue such as
position or a dashed line. The quick test is to view the chart in greyscale — if it still
reads, it will read for everyone.

And the most useful colour in data visualisation is grey. Put everything in grey, then use
one colour on the single thing your sentence is about. A chart with one accent is read
correctly in a second; a chart with nine has to be studied.

:::tip[The eye you already have]
When a chart feels wrong, it usually is wrong, and the reason is usually on this page: a
truncated axis, an area pretending to be a length, a hue pretending to be a quantity. Trust
that reaction and then go and find which one it was.
:::
