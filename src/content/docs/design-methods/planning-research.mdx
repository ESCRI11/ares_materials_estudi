*User Research Design* is built around three outcomes: writing a research plan and choosing
the right methods, running the research, and synthesising findings into insights you can
communicate. This lesson covers the first of those. The third has [its own
page](/ares_materials_estudi/design-methods/from-findings-to-insights/).

Note the course name. It is research *design* — the graded thinking is in the choices you
make before anyone is interviewed, not in your manner during the interview.

## From brief to research question

A brief states an intention: *make booking the workshop machines less painful*. It is not
answerable. A **research question** is a question about people and their situation that
evidence could settle, and that you do not already know the answer to.

Three tests, and a question has to pass all three:

1. **Could evidence change the answer?** "Would users like a nicer booking screen" fails —
   you know what they will say.
2. **Is it about the situation rather than the solution?** "Should the button be at the top"
   is a design question. You answer that by building two and testing, not by researching.
3. **Would a different answer change what you do next?** If both answers lead to the same
   design, do not spend two weeks on it.

The brief above turns into something like:

- How do students currently decide when to book, and what do they check first?
- What happens when a booking fails or a machine is occupied anyway?
- Who is involved besides the person booking — technicians, coursemates, staff?

Each of those is answerable, none of them presumes the outcome is a screen, and all three
would change the design depending on how they come out.

Two or three questions is a research plan. Nine is a wish list, and it will produce shallow
answers to all nine.

## Matching a method to the question

The unhelpful version of this is a list of methods. The useful version is a single
distinction: **what people say, what people do, and what people make** are three different
sources of evidence, and they disagree with each other constantly. People are unreliable
narrators of their own behaviour, not because they lie, but because habit is invisible from
the inside.

So the first move is deciding which of the three your question needs.

| Method | Answers | Cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **Semi-structured interview** — prepared topics, adapted live | Why people do what they do, how they explain and justify it, their history with the problem | What they actually do. You get the reconstructed version |
| **Contextual inquiry** — watching someone work in their own setting, asking as you go | The real sequence, the workarounds, the physical and social context | Motivation for things they never mention or notice |
| **Observation** — watching without intervening | What happens when nobody is being helpful for your benefit | Anything about intent |
| **Diary study** — participants record their own experience over days or weeks | Change over time, rare events, things that only make sense in sequence | Detail — self-reports are thin, and people drop out |
| **Survey** — same questions, many people | How common something is, once you already know what to ask about | Anything you did not think to ask. It measures, it does not discover |
| **Usability test** — a person attempts real tasks on something that exists | Where a specific design fails, and how people recover | Whether the thing is worth building at all |
| **Co-design session** — participants make something with prepared materials | Aspirations, tacit knowledge, what people would change if allowed | Frequency, or anything about the current system's actual use |

Two rules people learn the expensive way. **A survey is a converging instrument**, so it
belongs after exploratory work, not before it — you cannot write good options for a
multiple-choice question about something you have not yet observed. And **a usability test is
a second-diamond method**: it evaluates a solution. Running one when the real question is
whether the problem exists tells you only that your prototype's menu is confusing.

## Sampling and recruitment

**Sampling** is deciding who you need. **Recruitment** is finding them, which is the part
that eats your schedule — start it the day the plan is signed off, not the day before the
sessions.

Recruit on **behaviour, not demographics**. "Aged 20–30, studies design" describes a hundred
irrelevant people. "Has booked a machine at least twice this term, and has had at least one
booking go wrong" describes the eight who can answer your question. A **screener** is the
short set of questions that sorts one from the other, and it should filter on things people
have done, with a specific timeframe.

How many? For qualitative work, fewer than you think and more than one. Five to eight people
per distinct group is a normal starting point, and the honest stopping rule is
**saturation** — you stop when new sessions stop producing new material. If your fourth
interview is still surprising you, you are not finished. If your seventh is confirming, you
probably are. Surveys are the opposite: numbers only mean something at a size worth
calculating in advance.

Deliberately include **extreme users** — the person who uses the system daily and the person
who gave up on it. They make the constraints visible in a way the comfortable middle never
does. And keep track of who you did *not* reach, because that group is where your design
will fail first.

:::caution[Consent is a task in the session, not a form you attach]
[Collecting data](/ares_materials_estudi/data/collecting-data/) covers what consent has to
contain, why replacing names with P1 and P2 is pseudonymisation rather than anonymisation, and
when a study has to go past your supervisor before it starts. All of it applies here.

What is specific to a research session is that consent is spoken before it is filed. Before the
recorder starts, out loud: who you are, what this is for, who will see the recording, how long
you keep it, and that they can stop at any point without giving a reason. Then get it in writing.
Put it in the plan twice — once as a material, once as a line in the schedule, because a
participant reading a form is five minutes of the hour you booked.
:::

## What the plan document contains

Short — one to two pages. It exists so that everyone agrees on the question before the money
is spent, and so that in week six you can remember why you did it this way.

- **Background and decision.** What prompted this, and specifically *what decision the
  research is meant to inform*. If you cannot fill this field in, stop and find out.
- **Research questions.** Two or three, in the form above.
- **Method, and why.** Not only which, but why that one answers these questions and what it
  will miss. This is the paragraph the course title is pointing at.
- **Participants.** Criteria, how many, how you will find them, screener questions.
- **Schedule.** Recruitment, sessions, analysis, readout. Analysis always takes longer than
  people plan for.
- **Materials.** Discussion guide, consent form, task list, prototype, whatever the session
  needs.
- **Analysis approach.** How you will get from recordings to insights, and who is involved
  in that.
- **Output and audience.** What gets produced, and who has to act on it.
- **Assumptions and risks.** What you are taking for granted, and what happens if
  recruitment fails.

## The discussion guide

The plan says *what*; the guide says *how the hour goes*. Three habits carry most of the
quality:

**Ask about specific past events, not generalities.** "Tell me about the last time you
booked a machine" gives you an actual story with details in it. "How do you usually book
machines" gives you a tidy summary that has been smoothed by memory.

**Do not ask people to predict themselves.** "Would you use a feature that…" is answered by
politeness, not behaviour. Past behaviour is evidence; future intention is not.

**Leave the silence alone.** The most useful sentence in most interviews is the one that
comes after you fail to fill a pause. Prepare fewer questions than you think you need, and
plan to follow what you hear.
