A finished model is not a deliverable. The course asks for two things on top of it, and they
are aimed at different people.

A **technical drawing** is for whoever has to make, check or repair the object. It is
unambiguous, dimensioned, and deliberately not attractive.

A **render** or an **assembly animation** is for whoever has to understand or approve the
idea. It is persuasive, and it hides exactly the information the drawing exists to state.

Both come out of the same Fusion model, from different workspaces in the switcher at the top
left.

## Technical drawings

Switch the workspace from `Design` to `Drawing` and choose the option to create one from the
current design. Fusion opens a sheet — a page, with a frame — and asks you to place the
first view.

What goes on it:

- **A base view plus projected views.** Place one view, usually the front, then drag out the
  top and side views from it. They stay aligned with each other, which is the point of the
  convention: a dimension in one view lines up with the same feature in the next. An
  isometric view in a corner helps a reader who is not used to reading orthographic ones.
- **Section views**, where a plane cuts through the object to show what is inside. The only
  honest way to describe a wall thickness or an internal cavity.
- **Detail views**, a circled area enlarged, for something too small to dimension in place.
- **Dimensions**, added here in the drawing, not taken from the model. You place each one
  deliberately.
- **A title block**, the ruled box in the bottom corner: the part name, who drew it, the
  date, the scale, the units, the material, and a revision number. It is not bureaucracy. A
  drawing detached from its title block cannot be trusted, because nobody knows which version
  it is or what scale it was printed at.

Three conventions that make the difference between a drawing and a picture with numbers on
it:

- **Dimension what matters for making the part**, not everything. A reader looks for the
  numbers they need, and forty of them hides the six that count.
- **Never dimension the same feature twice.** Two numbers describing one distance is how a
  contradiction reaches a workshop.
- **State the projection convention and the units.** Europe uses **first-angle** projection
  and the rest of the world mostly uses **third-angle**; they place the side views on
  opposite sides of the drawing, and confusing them mirrors the part. The symbol in the title
  block says which is in use. Say millimetres out loud too.

The parametric payoff shows up here as well: the drawing stays linked to the model. Change a
dimension in the design and the views update, leaving you to check that your annotations
still sit sensibly. Export as **PDF** for sending to a person, or **DXF** if someone needs
the geometry back.

## Renders

Switch to the `Render` workspace and the same model appears in a lit studio.

Three things to set, and only three:

- **Appearances** — how a surface looks: brushed aluminium, matte black plastic, oak, frosted
  acrylic. Drag them onto a body or a face. Note that this is separate from the model's
  *physical material*, which is what Fusion uses to calculate weight — one is for looks, the
  other is for engineering, and they are set in different places.
- **Scene settings** — the environment lighting, the background, the ground plane and its
  shadow. The lighting comes from an image of a real environment wrapped around the scene, so
  choosing a different environment changes the whole mood in one click. Turning on a ground
  shadow is usually what makes an object stop floating.
- **The camera** — perspective or orthographic, and the focal length. A long lens flattens
  and looks like product photography; a short one exaggerates and looks like a phone snapshot
  taken too close. You already know this from photography, and it applies unchanged.

Then render — either live in the canvas, which refines the image while you watch, or on
Autodesk's servers, which is slower to start and better at the finish. Cloud rendering may
be unavailable on a personal-use licence; the local render is more than good enough for a
course presentation.

You have a design education, so the composition, the crop and the light are the part you will
find easy. Resist the default temptation of the perfect studio white background for
everything — a prototype photographed as if it already exists in the world usually argues
its case better.

## Assembly animations

The `Animation` workspace exists for one purpose: showing how the parts of a thing go
together.

You get a storyboard along the bottom and a model made of components. The two moves that
cover most needs:

- **Auto-explode**, which pulls every component apart along its own axis in one command. A
  serviceable exploded view in about ten seconds.
- **Manual transforms**, where you drag the timeline marker to a moment, move a component,
  and Fusion records that motion between the two positions. Build the sequence in the order
  the object is actually assembled.

Then publish it to a video file.

Keep it short — twenty to forty seconds, one idea. An assembly animation earns its place when
it shows something a still image genuinely cannot: how a joint engages, where a cable runs,
which way a lid comes off. If a photograph would have said the same thing, use the
photograph.

:::note[Components, not bodies]
Animation only moves **components** — the named parts of an assembly. If you modelled
everything as separate *bodies* inside one part, there is nothing for it to explode. Making
components as you go, and naming them, is a small habit that pays for itself the first time
you need this workspace.
:::
