**Fusion 360** is the CAD program the course uses. **CAD** stands for computer-aided design:
software for drawing an object precisely, at real dimensions, in a form a machine can
manufacture. Autodesk make it, and it is unusual in covering the whole chain in one window —
modelling, technical drawings, renders, animations, and the toolpaths for machines.

Autodesk have been dropping the "360" from the name, so you will see both *Fusion 360* and
plain *Fusion* in menus, tutorials and course material. Same program.

## Getting a licence without paying

Fusion is expensive at full price and free for you, by two separate routes. Both start in
the same place: go to **autodesk.com** and use their own search or menus.

- **Education licence.** Autodesk give students and educators a free licence for a year,
  renewable while you are enrolled. Look for the education or students section of the site.
  You have to prove you are a student, which usually means an institutional email address
  or a document from SUPSI — so this one becomes available once you are enrolled.
- **Personal-use licence.** A free, non-commercial version available to anyone, found in the
  Fusion product pages. Some features are switched off and the licence has to be renewed
  periodically, but everything in these lessons works on it.

Start on personal use now, and switch to the education licence when your SUPSI credentials
arrive. Nothing is lost in the change.

:::caution[Do not download Fusion from anywhere else]
Get the installer from autodesk.com after signing in, never from a search result promising
a free version. The paid-software-for-free corner of the internet is where malware lives,
and CAD tools are a favourite disguise.
:::

**One requirement to check before you plan anything:** Fusion runs on macOS and Windows.
There is no official Linux version. The current system requirements are listed on
autodesk.com — worth reading if your laptop is more than a few years old, because Fusion
leans on the graphics chip.

## Making an Autodesk account

You need an Autodesk account before you can install or run anything. It takes a few minutes:
an email address, a password, a confirmation link. Use an address you will still have in two
years, because your files are attached to it.

Then download the installer, run it, and sign in when Fusion opens. The first launch is slow.

## Cloud-based, and what that actually means

Most software you know saves a file into a folder you chose. Fusion does not, by default.

Your work lives in a **project** inside an Autodesk **hub** — storage on Autodesk's servers,
attached to your account. The practical effects:

- **You sign in to work.** Fusion tolerates being offline for a while and syncs later, but
  it is not a program you can use for a week on a plane.
- **Every save is a version, forever.** Save with a comment and Fusion keeps that state.
  You can open version 4 of a model from three weeks ago, or promote it back to current.
  It is the same idea as [a commit in git](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/what-version-control-is/),
  applied automatically to CAD, and it means you can experiment without fear.
- **Sharing is a link,** not an attachment. Useful when a tutor asks to see a model.
- **Local copies are exports, not the original.** `File → Export` writes a `.f3d` file onto
  your disk. Do that before any deadline, and before the end of a licence period. A cloud
  you do not administer is not a backup.

## Parametric, and what that actually means

A drawing program stores shapes. Fusion stores **the steps you took to make the shapes** —
a recipe, in order, with every number in it still editable.

Draw a rectangle 40 mm wide, pull it up 20 mm, round the corners. Fusion has recorded three
instructions, not one lump of geometry. Change the 40 to 55 tomorrow and the rectangle, the
solid and the rounded corners all rebuild around the new number.

This is the single biggest difference from every design tool you already use, and it gets a
[lesson of its own](/ares_materials_estudi/digital-fabrication/parametric-modelling/). For
now, know that it is why the interface has a timeline along the bottom.

## A tour of the window

Open Fusion and look at five regions.

**The Data Panel** — the grid of nine dots at the top left. It slides open to show your
projects and the files inside them, and it is how you create a project and open anything.
Close it again while modelling; it takes half the screen.

**The workspace switcher** — the leftmost drop-down in the toolbar, usually reading
**Design**. Fusion is really several programs stacked in one window, and this is how you
change which one you are in: `Design` for modelling, `Render` for pretty images, `Animation`
for exploded assembly videos, `Drawing` for dimensioned technical drawings, `Manufacture`
for machine toolpaths. You will spend nearly all your time in Design.

**The toolbar** — the row of icons across the top, grouped into tabs (`Solid`, `Surface`,
`Sheet Metal`, `Utilities`) and then into labelled drop-downs (`Create`, `Modify`,
`Assemble`, `Inspect`). Every command lives in one of those drop-downs. When a tutorial says
"extrude", it means `Solid → Create → Extrude`.

**The browser** — the small tree at the top left of the canvas, with an eye icon beside each
entry. This is the layers panel of CAD, and it holds more than you expect: `Document
Settings` (where the units live), `Origin` (the three reference planes and axes every model
starts from), then your `Sketches`, `Bodies` and `Components` as you create them. Clicking an
eye hides something without deleting it, exactly as in Illustrator or Photoshop.

**The timeline** — the strip of small icons along the bottom edge. Each icon is one operation
you performed, in the order you performed it. Double-click one to edit it. This is the
recipe, made visible, and it is the part of the interface that has no equivalent in the
tools you already know.

Also worth finding: the **ViewCube** at the top right, a small cube you click to snap to
front, top or an isometric view, and the **navigation bar** at the bottom centre, which
holds orbit, pan, zoom and `Fit`.

## Orbit, pan, zoom

With a normal mouse:

- **Scroll wheel** — zoom in and out, centred on the cursor.
- **Press the wheel and drag** — pan, sliding the model around the screen.
- **Shift + press the wheel and drag** — orbit, turning the model in space.
- **`F6`, or the `Fit` button** — frame everything, for when you have lost your model.

On a laptop trackpad, use the navigation bar at the bottom of the window instead, and
consider borrowing a mouse for a long session. In `Preferences` you can invert the zoom
direction and make the navigation mimic other CAD programs, which is worth doing on day one
if scrolling feels backwards to you. It will feel awkward for about a week and then become
invisible.

:::note[Set your units before you draw]
In the browser, right-click `Document Settings` (or open the units entry underneath it) and
confirm you are in **millimetres**. Every Fablab machine in Europe works in millimetres, and
a model built in inches arrives at the printer at 25.4 times the size you meant.
:::

- [ ] I have an Autodesk account and can sign in on autodesk.com
- [ ] I found the free education or personal-use licence from autodesk.com, without using a
      download link from anywhere else
- [ ] Fusion is installed and opens with me signed in
- [ ] I created a project in the Data Panel and gave it a name
- [ ] I can open and close the Data Panel without losing my model
- [ ] I found the workspace switcher and can name what `Design`, `Render`, `Drawing` and
      `Animation` are for

- [ ] I can zoom, pan and orbit without thinking about which key does which
- [ ] I clicked the ViewCube corner to get an isometric view, and pressed `Fit` to reframe
- [ ] I saved the empty file with a name and a comment, and found it again in the Data Panel
