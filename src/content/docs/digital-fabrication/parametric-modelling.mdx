In Illustrator you draw a rectangle and a rectangle exists. In Fusion you draw a rectangle
and Fusion writes down *"a rectangle, 40 mm by 20 mm, on this plane, its corner at the
origin"*. The rectangle on screen is the result of that sentence, not the thing itself.

This is what **parametric** means: the model is made of parameters — named, editable
numbers and rules — and the geometry is recalculated from them whenever one changes. You
are not drawing an object. You are recording the instructions for building it, and watching
the current result.

Everything else in this lesson follows from that one idea. It is also the reason the first
week of CAD feels slower than drawing: you are typing dimensions where you used to drag
handles. The payback arrives the first time a tutor asks what it looks like 8 mm taller and
the answer takes four seconds.

## The loop

Nearly every part is made by repeating three steps.

1. **Sketch** — draw a closed 2D outline on a flat plane.
2. **Constrain** — lock that outline down with rules and dimensions until it cannot move.
3. **Extrude** — give the outline depth, turning it into a solid.

Then sketch on a face of what you built, and go round again. A complicated object is a long
sequence of simple loops, not one difficult operation.

## What a sketch plane is

A **sketch** is a 2D drawing, and it always lives on a flat surface in 3D space. Choosing
that surface is the first thing Fusion asks you for.

It is an artboard you can place anywhere in space. Three of them exist before you start,
in the `Origin` folder of the browser: the front, top and right planes, all crossing at a
single point called the **origin**. You can also sketch on any flat face of a solid you have
already made — the top of a box, so the next thing you draw sits exactly there.

Two habits, learned the hard way by everyone:

- **Start at the origin.** Your first sketch should touch it, ideally symmetrically about
  it. A model floating a random distance from the origin fights you later, in mirroring, in
  assembly, and in every drawing view.
- **Prefer the origin planes to the faces of your own geometry.** A sketch drawn on a face
  depends on that face continuing to exist. Delete or move the feature that made it and the
  sketch loses its home, taking everything built on it along. This is the most common way a
  Fusion model falls apart, and it has nothing to do with skill.

## Constraints, and why snapping is not enough

You already know **snapping**: you drag an edge, it clicks to an alignment, you let go, it
looks right. Snapping is a one-time favour. Nothing remembers that the two edges were meant
to be aligned, and the next edit can quietly break it.

A **constraint** is a rule that holds. Apply *horizontal* to a line and it is horizontal
now and after every subsequent change. The usual set, all of which you already understand
as design ideas:

- **Horizontal / vertical** — a line stays flat or upright.
- **Coincident** — two points are the same point. This is what actually attaches things.
- **Parallel, perpendicular, tangent** — the relationships between two lines or a line and
  an arc.
- **Equal** — two lines or two circles are always the same size as each other, whatever
  that size turns out to be.
- **Concentric** — two circles share a centre.
- **Symmetric** — two elements mirror each other about a line. Enormously useful, and
  underused by beginners.

A **dimension** is a constraint with a number: this line is 40 mm; these two holes are 25 mm
apart; this angle is 30 degrees. Add one with the dimension tool, type the value, press
Enter.

The difference in practice: dimensions say how big, constraints say how things relate. If a
relationship should survive a change of size, it wants a constraint, not two dimensions that
happen to match.

## Fully constrained is the goal

Fusion colours sketch geometry to tell you its status. Under-constrained geometry is drawn
in **blue** and you can drag it around with the mouse. Once every element is pinned by rules
and dimensions it turns **black**, and dragging does nothing.

That state is **fully constrained**, and it is what you are aiming for before you leave any
sketch.

Not for tidiness. A blue sketch has freedom in it that you did not decide — a rectangle that
is 40 mm wide today because that is where you released the mouse, not because you chose 40.
Change something upstream and that undecided freedom gets used up in whatever way the solver
finds convenient, which is how a model develops a 39.7 mm edge nobody drew.

Two ways to close the gap when a sketch stubbornly stays blue:

- Drag the geometry. Whatever moves is what is still free.
- Look for the missing *relationship* rather than adding another number. Beginners add too
  many dimensions; a symmetric constraint often replaces three of them and describes the
  intent better.

If Fusion refuses a constraint and says the sketch is over-constrained, you have told it the
same thing twice. Remove one, do not fight it.

## Extrude, and the other features

To **extrude** is to take a closed 2D profile and push it perpendicular to its plane to make
a solid — the CAD verb for giving a shape thickness. Select the region, type a distance,
press Enter.

The setting that matters is the **operation**, on the extrude dialogue:

- **New body** — makes a separate solid.
- **Join** — fuses with what is already there.
- **Cut** — removes material instead of adding it. This is how holes, slots and pockets are
  made: sketch the shape of the hole on a face, extrude it as a cut.

Once a solid exists, several features need no sketch at all:

- **Fillet** rounds an edge; **chamfer** cuts it at 45 degrees. Both are chosen by clicking
  the edge and typing a radius or a distance.
- **Shell** hollows a solid out to a wall thickness, which turns a block into a box in one
  operation. Learn this one early; it is most of what an enclosure is.
- **Hole** places a properly specified hole, including the counterbores and threads you get
  in real hardware.
- **Revolve** spins a profile around an axis, for anything turned: a knob, a bottle, a lens
  housing.

Keep fillets and chamfers late in the sequence. They are decoration on top of the shape, and
having them early makes every later edit harder for the solver to rebuild.

## The timeline is an editable history

The strip along the bottom of the window holds one icon per operation, left to right, in the
order you did them. That is the recipe, and it is not read-only.

- **Double-click a feature** to reopen its dialogue. Change 40 to 55, press Enter, and the
  model rebuilds — including everything you made after it. The hole you cut, the shell, the
  fillets: all recomputed against the new number.
- **Double-click a sketch** in the timeline or the browser to edit its dimensions and
  constraints directly.
- **Drag the marker** at the end of the timeline backwards to travel to an earlier state and
  see the model part-built. Anything you create with the marker parked there is inserted at
  that point in the sequence rather than at the end.
- **Right-click a feature** to suppress, reorder or delete it.

This is the whole point of parametric CAD, so it is worth being explicit about what you have
gained: **you can change a decision made an hour ago without redoing the work built on top
of it.** No tool in your existing kit does this. In Illustrator, changing the width of a
shape you drew first thing does nothing to the twenty things you aligned to it afterwards.

The cost is that the recipe has to remain valid. If you delete the face a later sketch was
drawn on, or move a hole so far it falls off the edge of the part, the affected features get
a warning marker and the model stops rebuilding until you fix them. Fusion tells you which
feature broke; the fix is nearly always to point the orphaned sketch at a plane that still
exists.

## Named parameters, the real payoff

You can go one step further and give numbers names. Under `Modify → Change Parameters`,
create a **user parameter**: a name, a value, a unit, and a comment.

```
material_thickness = 3 mm
box_width         = 120 mm
sensor_hole       = 16.2 mm
```

Now type `material_thickness` into a dimension field instead of `3`. Fusion accepts names
and arithmetic anywhere it accepts a number, so `material_thickness * 2` and
`box_width / 3` are valid dimensions too.

When the Fablab hands you plywood that measures 2.8 mm rather than the 3 mm on the label,
you change one parameter and every slot, tab and finger joint in the model adjusts. Without
named parameters, that is forty dimensions found by hand, and you will miss two.

Name parameters for anything that comes from the physical world — material thickness,
component sizes, the fit clearance between two parts — and anything you already suspect you
will argue about.

      model without it
