You can already see, and this is not a lesson on composition. It is a short list of the things
that are different about photographing a half-built electronic object rather than a finished
product, and they are mostly problems of *timing* and *legibility* rather than of taste.

## What makes a prototype awkward

**It is unfinished on purpose.** There will be jumper wires, tape, a breadboard, a USB cable
going somewhere. The instinct to hide all of that produces a photograph that lies about what
stage the work is at. The instinct to accept a cluttered frame produces one nobody can read.
The middle is: show the mess that is the project, remove the mess that is your desk.

**It has no scale.** A microcontroller board photographed alone could be the size of a stamp or
a book. A hand in the frame fixes this in one shot and nothing else does as well.

**It emits light.** An LED, a screen or a strip will be several stops brighter than the object
around it, and a camera exposing for the object turns the light into a white blob with no colour.

**It only makes sense in use.** An interactive object sitting on a table is a photograph of a
lump. The interaction is the design, and it needs a hand in it.

**It is temporary.** The circuit goes back in the kit, the enclosure gets glued, the prototype
gets cannibalised for the next one. Some photographs have a window of one afternoon.

## The five shots

Every physical project owes you these. Take them all, on the day.

1. **The whole object, plain background.** The identifying shot — the one that goes at the top
   of the project page and in your portfolio. Frame so that the crop cuts off wires heading
   offstage rather than leaving them trailing across the picture.
2. **In use, or in the hand.** Solves scale and purpose in the same frame. Borrow someone
   else's hands so yours can hold the camera.
3. **The detail.** The joint, the connector, the sensor, the thing you spent a day on. Close
   subjects have very little depth of field, so decide which millimetre is sharp before you
   press, or take several and choose later.
4. **The inside, before it closes.** Wiring, board placement, how it is held together. This is
   the shot that cannot be retaken, and it is the one people forget.
5. **The failure.** The warped print, the misaligned cut, version one next to version three.
   These are the pictures the [build log](/ares_materials_estudi/documentation/what-documentation-is-for/)
   exists to keep, and the ones a marker reads most carefully.

## Light

Three specifics for objects like these; the rest you already know.

**One big soft source, plus a fill.** A window with the object close to it, and a sheet of white
card or paper on the opposite side to lift the shadow. Two hard sources give two shadows in
different directions, which is the fastest way to make an object look like a snapshot.

**Turn the room light off when you use the window.** Daylight and a warm bulb in one frame
leave a colour cast that cannot be corrected in a single move, because the two casts sit in
different parts of the picture. You know this; it happens in a workshop far more than in a
studio, because the workshop lights are always on.

**An LED will blow out.** If you expose so the object reads, the light is a featureless white
patch. Two ways out:

- Shoot in a dim room and expose for the light, accepting that the object goes dark around it.
  A glowing thing in the dark is often the better picture anyway.
- Take two frames from the same position — one exposed for the object, one for the light — and
  use both in the document, side by side. Two honest photographs beat one compromised.

Turning the brightness down in code before shooting is legitimate and often the easiest fix.

## Background

Plain is the safe default: a sheet of paper curving up the wall, a piece of MDF, a painted wall.
It isolates the object and it never fights.

Context is frequently the better choice for something interactive — a desk, a doorway, a
kitchen. The point made in
[drawings and renders](/ares_materials_estudi/digital-fabrication/drawings-and-renders/) about
resisting the studio-white default applies to photographs at least as much as to renders: an
object shown where it would be used argues its own case.

What is never right is the workshop behind the object by accident. Either the environment is
chosen or it is removed.

## Screens are screenshots

Do not photograph a screen. You get moiré, reflections, a colour shift and a dark band across
it from the refresh. Take a screenshot and place it in the document instead — that is what
screenshots are named as a deliverable for.

The exception is when the object and its screen have to be in one frame to make sense. Then:
lower the screen brightness, and use a shutter of 1/60 s or slower, because a faster one catches
the display mid-refresh and leaves the band.

## Practical matters

- **Shoot on the day.** Not at the end of the project, and not once for everything.
- **Take five times what you need.** Deleting is free; going back is not possible.
- **A phone is fine.** A recent phone in good light beats a borrowed camera you have not set up,
  and this is not the week to learn a new body.
- **Put it on something.** A tripod, a stack of books, a shelf. Add the two-second timer and the
  shake disappears.
- **Name files as you copy them off.** `2026-10-14-enclosure-v2-open.jpg` sorts chronologically
  and says what it is. `IMG_4471.jpg` is a file you will open twelve of to find one.
- **Keep the originals out of the repository.** Raw files and full-resolution exports are exactly
  what [.gitignore](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/what-not-to-commit/) is for. Export a
  web copy — around 2000 px on the long edge, comfortably under 500 KB — for the document, and
  keep the originals on a drive.

## Do this now

You do not need the kit for this. Photograph something you have already made, or a mock-up of
one — a box with a cable coming out of it will do for practice.

- [ ] I photographed an object whole, on a plain background, with nothing of my desk in frame
- [ ] I took a second shot with a hand in it, so the scale is unambiguous
- [ ] I took a detail shot and checked at full size that the part I cared about is sharp
- [ ] I took an "inside" shot before closing or covering anything
- [ ] I photographed something that went wrong, and kept it
- [ ] I used one soft light source and something white opposite it as fill
- [ ] I turned the room light off while shooting by a window, and compared the two versions
- [ ] I made a bright light in the frame and shot it twice — once for the object, once for the light
- [ ] I renamed the files by date and description as I copied them off the camera
- [ ] I exported a web-sized copy and left the originals outside the repository
