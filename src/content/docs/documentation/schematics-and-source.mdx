Of the five deliverables, three sit close to what a design education already gave you. Two do
not: **the hardware schematics** and **the software source files**. Both are technical documents
with a right answer, which — once you know what the right answer is — makes them the easiest
two to get full marks on.

## A photograph of a breadboard is not a schematic

It is a useful photograph. It is not the document that was asked for, for four reasons:

- **Wires hide each other.** Jumper wires cross, arch and sit on top of one another, and a
  photograph flattens all of that into one plane.
- **It cannot say which hole.** Two adjacent rows on a breadboard are electrically strangers,
  and at photograph resolution they are one pixel apart.
- **It does not state values.** A 220 Ω and a 10 kΩ resistor are the same object with different
  stripes, and the stripes are not legible at any size you will publish.
- **It cannot say which pin.** `GP15` and `GP16` are next to each other on the board and print
  in 1 mm type.

The test, which the rest of this lesson keeps coming back to: **could a competent stranger
rebuild this circuit from this page, without asking you anything?** If not, it is a photograph
of a circuit, which is a different and lesser thing.

A **schematic** states connection and ignores position. The conventions for reading one — the
dot at a junction, the crossing without a dot, every ground symbol being the same point — are in
[circuits and breadboards](/ares_materials_estudi/physical-computing/circuits-and-breadboards/).
This lesson is about producing one.

## What yours has to state

Whatever tool you use, these five things have to be recoverable from the drawing:

1. **Every component, named, with its value or part number.** `220 Ω resistor`, `SG90 servo`,
   `Raspberry Pi Pico`. Not "resistor".
2. **Every connection**, including the ones that feel too obvious to draw.
3. **Which pin on the board**, by its printed label rather than "the third one down".
4. **The power source and its voltage.** Where the energy enters.
5. **The shared ground.** If a motor or a strip has its own supply, its ground still has to meet
   the board's ground, and leaving that line off the drawing is the most common omission there
   is.

Alongside the drawing goes a **bill of materials** — often shortened to BOM — a list of every
part with its quantity and value, so that someone could order the parts without measuring your
prototype. A markdown table is the whole job:

```md
| Qty | Part | Value / model |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Microcontroller board | Raspberry Pi Pico |
| 1 | LED | 5 mm red |
| 1 | Resistor | 220 Ω |
| 1 | Pushbutton | 6 mm tactile |
```

## Three honest ways to produce one

### 1. Out of Wokwi

You will have built every circuit in
[physical computing](/ares_materials_estudi/physical-computing/circuits-and-breadboards/) in
**Wokwi**, the browser simulator. A Wokwi project is two files: your code, and a file called
`diagram.json` — the wiring, stored as text. Every part, every wire and every pin is in there,
which is why an earlier lesson could turn off a button's contact bounce by editing one line of
it.

That gives you two artefacts for no extra work:

- **A picture.** The diagram panel is an exact, labelled drawing of the circuit. Capture it with
  the screenshot shortcut from
  [the previous lesson](/ares_materials_estudi/documentation/screen-and-video/). Be accurate
  about what it is when you submit it: this is a **wiring diagram** — it draws the parts where
  they physically sit — rather than a symbolic schematic. For a course project with a dozen
  connections, presented together with the bill of materials and a pin table, that is honest and
  sufficient.
- **A file you can commit.** `diagram.json` goes in the repository next to the code. It is text,
  so git can show what changed in it, and your wiring gets a history like everything else. Better
  still, anyone can load it back into Wokwi and *run* your circuit without owning a single
  component — which is a stronger deliverable than any drawing.

One honesty requirement: Wokwi's parts are not always the parts in your kit. Where the simulated
component differs from the one in your hand, say so in the README rather than letting the
diagram imply otherwise.

### 2. A drawing tool

- **Fritzing** draws the same circuit three ways — as a breadboard, as a schematic, and as a
  circuit board — and switching between the first two views is how the pictorial version and the
  symbolic version finally connect for most people. It asks a small payment for the ready-built
  download.
- **KiCad** is free, professional, and what real electronics is designed in. It is far more tool
  than a semester-one project needs, and a genuine skill if you want it later.

Neither is required. Do not start learning KiCad the week before a hand-in.

### 3. By hand

A ruled pen drawing, labelled, photographed square and evenly lit, is a legitimate schematic —
it was the professional format for a century. It fails on exactly two things: illegibility, and
private symbols. Use the standard ones from the circuits lesson, write every value next to its
component, and it stands up.

### The minimum that always works: a pin table

Even with no drawing at all, this table answers most of what a reader needs, and it is written
in the [Markdown](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/markdown/) you already have:

```md
| From | To | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `GP15` | pushbutton, leg 1 | other leg to `GND` |
| `GP16` | LED anode, via 220 Ω resistor | cathode to `GND` |
| `GP26` | potentiometer, wiper | outer legs to `3V3` and `GND` |
| `3V3` | sensor `VCC` | |
| `GND` | sensor `GND` | shared with the board |
```

It is not a substitute for a drawing when a drawing is what was asked for. It is text, so git
tracks it, a reader can copy it, and it removes every ambiguity a photograph leaves behind. Put
one on every project page you write.

## What "software source files" means

It means the files you edit. Not a PDF of the code, not screenshots of the editor, not a zip
called `project_final_final2`.

Two ways it goes wrong:

- **Readable but not runnable** — code pasted into a document, so a reader can see it and cannot
  execute it.
- **Runnable only on your machine** — a folder that works because of something installed on your
  laptop that is written down nowhere.

The answer is one you already have from
[the git section](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/github/): a repository, with a
[README](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/markdown/) saying what it is, how to run it, and
what it is made of. Push it, and hand in the link.

For one of these projects, the repository holds:

- the code — `main.py`, or the HTML, CSS and JavaScript
- `diagram.json`, or the schematic source, plus an exported image of it
- the CAD source file *and* the exported `.stl` or `.dxf` — the source as well as the export,
  because an STL cannot be edited back into a design
- the README: description, bill of materials, pin table, how to run it, a link to the video
- an `images/` folder with the web-sized photographs
- a [.gitignore](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/what-not-to-commit/) keeping the raw
  photographs, the video export and the renders out

And the test one more time: send the link to a classmate who was not in the room. Whatever they
have to ask you is the thing the documentation is missing.
