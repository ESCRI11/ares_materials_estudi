Two of the deliverables live in moving pictures: **screenshots and screen recordings** of
anything digital, and **a video that tells the story of the project**. They are different jobs.
A screen recording proves something works. The project video explains why it exists.

## Capturing the screen

Everything you need is already installed.

**Windows.** `Win` + `Shift` + `S` opens the Snipping Tool overlay: drag a region for a still,
or switch it to its recording mode for video of that region. `Win` + `G` opens the Game Bar,
which records the active window and is the more reliable of the two for anything longer than a
few seconds.

**macOS.** `Cmd` + `Shift` + `4` for a region still, `Cmd` + `Shift` + `5` for the recorder,
which offers the whole screen or a selection.

**More than that**, and the standard free answer is OBS Studio, which records several sources at
once — screen, camera, microphone. Worth installing only if you need a face or a camera angle
composited with the screen.

Five things to do before you press record:

- **Silence notifications.** Do Not Disturb on either system. A message sliding in over your
  demo is the single most common reason a recording gets redone.
- **Clean the frame.** Hide the bookmarks bar, close the twenty tabs, use an empty desktop. A
  personal browser window in a submitted recording is a small permanent embarrassment.
- **Make the text bigger.** Editor and terminal text at your normal size is unreadable once the
  video is scaled down into a page. Two or three steps up.
- **Record the window, not the whole desktop.** Cropping afterwards throws away resolution.
- **Move the cursor slowly and deliberately.** The viewer follows the pointer. Pause for a beat
  after each click so they can see what changed.

Then cut the loading, the typos and the pauses. A screen recording that earns its place is
usually under thirty seconds.

## The project video

The syllabus asks for a video that *tells the story of the project*. Story means it has an
order, and the order is the same one every time.

Four beats, fifteen to thirty seconds each:

1. **What it is.** The object, working, in one clean shot. The first ten seconds decide whether
   the rest gets watched.
2. **What it does.** Someone using it. Their hand, and the response.
3. **How it works.** The inside, the schematic on screen, a sensor value moving, the mechanism.
4. **What you learned.** The version that failed, the thing you would change. Twenty seconds,
   and it is what separates documentation from a demo reel.

**Sixty to a hundred and twenty seconds** for the whole thing. Ninety is usually right. Long
videos are not watched to the end, and the fourth beat is at the end.

## Show the interaction, do not narrate it

The rule that fixes most student videos: **if the camera can show it, the voice should not say
it.** A shot of a hand pressing a button, over a voice saying "the user presses the button", is
dead air twice over.

Use the voice for what the picture cannot carry: why a button rather than a gesture, what the
threshold is set to, what surprised you.

The hardest shot in the whole exercise is the hand and the response in one frame. Three ways
through it:

- Frame wide enough that both are in shot, and accept that neither is large.
- Cut — the press in one shot, the response in the next — and cut *on* the action, so the timing
  reads as continuous.
- If the response is on a screen, record the screen separately and place the two side by side in
  the edit. This is usually the clearest and it is the least work.

## Filming the object

- **Lock the camera off.** A tripod, or books. Handheld footage of a small object is unusable.
- **Two people.** One uses the object, one operates the camera. Doing both alone produces a
  video of your own arm.
- **Shoot each action three times.** Editing is choosing, and one take gives you nothing to
  choose between.
- **Get b-roll** — the supporting footage you cut away to when a shot runs short. Slow moves
  across the object, close-ups of a connector, the LED in a dark room. Five minutes of it saves
  the edit.
- **Record the object's own sound** if it has one: a servo, a relay, a click. Sync sound is what
  makes a physical object feel real on screen.
- **Do not put music under speech** unless you can lower the music where the voice sits.
  Otherwise both become unintelligible.

## Voice, captions and text

**Record the voiceover separately**, in a quiet room, phone about 20 cm away, reading from a
script you wrote down. Recording it live over the shot gives you room echo, camera noise and a
sentence you cannot re-record without reshooting.

**Or use no voice at all.** Title cards between the four beats, sync sound underneath. This is
faster, it is often stronger, and it removes the accent question entirely.

**Captions are not optional.** The video will be watched on a phone with the sound off, and by
people whose first language is not English. Most editors will generate them automatically now —
read every line before you export, because they will confidently mistranscribe every technical
word in the project.

Text on screen has to be readable on a phone: large, high contrast, two lines at most, on screen
long enough to read twice.

## Exporting and where it lives

**1080p, MP4 with H.264, 30 frames per second.** Higher settings make a bigger file that looks
the same in the page it is embedded in.

The file does not go in the repository. GitHub warns above 50 MB and
[refuses anything over 100 MB](/ares_materials_estudi/git-and-github/what-not-to-commit/), and a
video committed once stays in the history forever. Upload it to a video platform or a shared
drive, set the sharing so that anyone with the link can watch, and put the link in the README.

Then **open that link in a private browser window**. A video nobody can open is a video that was
not submitted, and permission settings default to private more often than you would expect.

## Do this now

None of this waits for the semester. Use anything on your desk as the subject — a lamp, a
kettle, a door.

- [ ] I wrote the four beats of a video as four lines before shooting anything
