Two of the courses you take in semester 1 are called **Documentation and Communication**.
One of them, ID120.02, sits inside the Digital Fabrication module; the other, ID140.02, sits
inside Physical Computing. They are 24 hours each — 48 hours of your first semester, which is
more time than the syllabus gives to most single subjects.

The syllabus says of ID140.02, in plain words: *in order to assess this module, it is
mandatory to submit the project documentation.* Both modules are worth 5 **ECTS** — the credit
unit European universities count degrees in — and neither can be passed without the document.

So this is not the write-up of the work. It is part of the work, marked alongside the object.

## What is actually asked for

Five things, across the two courses:

- **A written project description.**
- **Visual documentation** — *screenshots* for anything that lives on a screen, *photographs*
  for anything physical.
- **A video that tells the story of the project.**
- **The software source files.**
- **The hardware schematics.**

Everything has to follow the guidelines each course hands out, and everything has to be good
enough to sit in a professional portfolio afterwards. That second requirement is not decoration:
it is the difference between a folder you submit and forget, and four projects you can show
someone eighteen months later.

Where the course guidelines differ from anything in this section, follow the course. This section
exists so that none of those five words is unfamiliar when you meet them.

:::note[Three of these are nearly home ground]
You have a design degree. Photographs, video and a written description are closer to what you
already do than anything else on this site, and these lessons will not teach you composition.
What is new is documenting a *technical* project: a drawing of a circuit, a handover of source
files, a demo of something that only makes sense when a hand touches it.
:::

## The log and the reconstruction

There are two ways to arrive at a finished document, and they are not equally hard.

**The reconstruction** is written in the two days before the deadline, from memory and from
whatever photographs your phone happened to keep. It is the normal way, it takes a miserable
weekend, and it is always thinner than the project was.

**The build log** is a folder and a text file. At the end of each working session — ten minutes,
not more — you write down what you tried, what happened, and what you changed, and you drop in
the photographs you took that day.

The log wins for a reason that has nothing to do with discipline: **half the material only
exists while the work is happening.**

- The photograph of the inside of the enclosure cannot be taken after you glue the lid on.
- The circuit is in the simulator this week and dismantled back into the kit next week.
- The two days you lost to a sensor that read nonsense is the most interesting page in the
  finished document, and it is the page you will have completely forgotten by December.
- The value you finally settled on — 220 Ω, a 40 ms debounce, 0.6 mm of clearance — is
  unrecoverable once the thing works. Nobody remembers a number that stopped being a problem.

A log entry does not need to be well written. Mine is a date, three lines, and two photographs.

```
2026-10-14
Servo jittering whenever the LED strip was on. Suspected the shared supply.
Gave the strip its own battery pack, kept a common ground to the board. Fixed.
Photos: servo-jitter-before.mp4, wiring-two-supplies.jpg
```

The log is not the document, and nobody will read it. It is the raw material the document gets
made out of, and having it turns a miserable weekend into an afternoon of editing.

## Who reads it, and for what

Two readers, and they want different things from the same pages.

**The marker** reads for evidence that you understand your own project. Why this sensor and not
the other one, why the joint is that shape, what you did when it did not work. A polished object
with no reasoning behind it is indistinguishable from a lucky one, and the documentation is the
only place the reasoning can appear. This is also why the failures belong in it — a problem you
diagnosed and solved is the strongest thing you can show, and hiding it throws away your best
evidence.

**A studio, later** reads for whether you can be handed a problem and come back with something.
They skim: what is it, what does it do, could I have built this from what is on this page. They
look at three projects and stop. The one with a video and a schematic beats the one with four
paragraphs of intent.

Those two readings pull in slightly different directions, which is why the deliverables are
split the way they are. The description and the build log carry the *why*, for the marker. The
video, the photographs and the schematic carry the *what*, for everyone else.

## Do this now

Before you build anything, decide where the log lives — a text file in the project folder is
enough.

- [ ] I know that two modules in semester 1 are assessed on documentation, not only on the object
- [ ] I can name the five deliverables: description, screenshots or photographs, video, source
      files, schematics
- [ ] I made a project folder with a `log.md` file in it
- [ ] I understand that a failure I diagnosed belongs in the document rather than being hidden
