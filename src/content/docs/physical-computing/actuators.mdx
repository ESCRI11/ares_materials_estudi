An **actuator** is the opposite of a sensor: a component that turns an electrical signal into
a physical effect. Light, movement, sound, heat, vibration. Sensors are how a project notices;
actuators are how it answers.

The problem is that a digital pin has two states, and most of what you want to do is not
binary. Half brightness, forty-five degrees, a note rather than a click.

## PWM: two states, used cleverly

**Pulse width modulation**, always written **PWM**, is the standard answer. Instead of holding
the pin high, you switch it on and off very fast and change the *proportion* of each cycle that
it spends on. That proportion is the **duty cycle**. A pin at 25 percent duty delivers a
quarter of the energy, and an LED driven that way looks about a quarter as bright, because your
eye averages a flicker it cannot resolve.

This is the same effect as the sleepless blink loop in lesson 5, done deliberately and by
hardware rather than by your code.

Two numbers describe a PWM signal:

- **Frequency** — how many on-off cycles per second. For an LED, 1000 Hz is high enough that
  nothing flickers visibly. It does not affect brightness.
- **Duty cycle** — the fraction of each cycle spent on. This is the one you vary.

```python
from machine import Pin, PWM
import time

led = PWM(Pin(15))
led.freq(1000)

while True:
    for level in range(0, 65536, 1000):
        led.duty_u16(level)
        time.sleep_ms(10)
```

`duty_u16()` takes 0 to 65535, the same range `read_u16()` produced in the last lesson —
which means a knob can drive a lamp with no conversion at all:

```python
led.duty_u16(knob.read_u16())
```

:::note[The fade will not look linear]
Perceived brightness does not follow duty cycle. The bottom of the range looks like most of
the change and the top half looks nearly identical throughout. That is your eye, not a bug.
Squaring the value before sending it — `level * level // 65535` — gives a fade that looks
much more even.
:::

Two practical notes. PWM is a voltage that switches, not a lower steady voltage: a meter
averages it, an oscilloscope sees a square wave. And on some chips neighbouring pins share one
PWM timer, so setting the frequency on one silently changes another. If two PWM outputs
interfere in a way that makes no sense, that is usually why.

## A servo

A **servo** is a small geared motor with electronics inside that hold it at a commanded
position. You do not tell it to turn; you tell it where to be, and it goes there and stays.
Three wires: power, ground, and one signal.

The signal is PWM, but a servo does not read the duty cycle the way an LED does. It measures
the **length of the on-pulse**: roughly 1 millisecond for one end of its travel, roughly 2 ms
for the other, repeated 50 times a second. Which means you set the frequency to 50 Hz and then
work out the duty value that produces the pulse width you want.

At 50 Hz each cycle is 20 ms, so a 1.5 ms pulse is 1.5/20 of the cycle, and
`65535 × 1.5 / 20 ≈ 4915`.

```python
from machine import Pin, PWM
import time

servo = PWM(Pin(16))
servo.freq(50)

MIN_US = 500    # pulse in microseconds for one end of the travel — tune this
MAX_US = 2500   # pulse for the other end — tune this too

def angle(degrees):
    us = MIN_US + (MAX_US - MIN_US) * degrees / 180
    servo.duty_u16(int(us * 65535 / 20000))

while True:
    angle(0)
    time.sleep(1)
    angle(90)
    time.sleep(1)
    angle(180)
    time.sleep(1)
```

`MIN_US` and `MAX_US` are named and adjustable on purpose. Servos vary: 500 and 2500 suit many,
1000 and 2000 suit others, and pushing a servo past its real limit makes it buzz and strain
against its own end stop. On real hardware, start with a narrow range, widen it until the arm
reaches the ends cleanly, and stop there. Anything that reads a physical position will need
calibrating like this.

## A buzzer

There are two kinds and they behave completely differently.

An **active buzzer** contains its own oscillator. Give it power and it makes its one note.
It is a digital output: `value(1)` for sound, `value(0)` for silence, no PWM involved.

A **passive buzzer** is a tiny speaker with no electronics. It makes whatever frequency you
feed it, which means you can play notes:

```python
from machine import Pin, PWM
import time

buzzer = PWM(Pin(17))

def tone(hz, ms):
    buzzer.freq(hz)
    buzzer.duty_u16(32768)   # half on, half off
    time.sleep_ms(ms)
    buzzer.duty_u16(0)       # silence

for note in (262, 294, 330, 349):
    tone(note, 300)
    time.sleep_ms(50)
```

Here the frequency carries the meaning and the duty cycle only needs to be somewhere in the
middle. Setting duty to 0 stops the sound; `buzzer.deinit()` releases the pin entirely when
you are finished with it.

## Why motors need help

You can drive an LED from a pin. You cannot drive a motor from one, and the reason is worth
understanding because it generalises to almost every powerful thing you will ever attach.

**Current.** A pin can supply milliamps. A small DC motor wants hundreds of milliamps while
running and considerably more at the instant it starts or stalls. The pin cannot deliver it,
and asking it to will at best brown out the board and at worst destroy the pin.

**Electrical noise.** A motor is a coil, and a coil resists having its current stopped: when
you switch it off it produces a voltage spike in the opposite direction that can be many times
the supply. That spike goes looking for somewhere to go, and a microcontroller pin is a bad
place for it to find.

The principle: **the pin carries the decision, not the power.** Between the pin and anything
hungry you put a switching component — a transistor, or a ready-made **motor driver** board —
which takes the small signal from the pin and uses it to control current from a separate,
larger supply. The two supplies must share a common ground, or neither side can tell what the
other's signals mean.

A servo sits on the edge of this rule. Its signal wire is genuinely only a signal, so that
part is fine; its power wire draws far more than a pin can give, so on a real board it goes to
a supply rather than to a data pin. In the simulator none of this matters, which is precisely
why it is worth reading now rather than discovering later.

:::caution[Before you wire a motor in class]
Ask what the motor draws and what your driver is rated for, and check the board's own limits
in its documentation. This is the one area of this section where a confident guess costs
money. Principle first, datasheet second, wire third.
:::
